# Interviews & Dialogue — Transcript-Driven Editing & Breathing Room

> The reference for interview, talking-head, podcast, documentary-dialogue, and any
> transcript-driven project. Covers building from the transcript, removing filler,
> and — critically — **breathing room** (the pauses that make an edit feel human).
> Builds on `kb/fundamentals.md`.

## The transcript is the edit

For dialogue work, **you edit the words first, the picture second.** Workflow:

1. **Get a word-level transcript** with timestamps (and speaker labels for multi-person).
2. **Read it as a script.** Find the story: the strongest answers, the throughline,
   the soundbites that carry meaning. Mark keepers; mark cuts.
3. **Assemble a "radio edit"** — a cut that works with eyes closed. If the audio
   story flows, the picture edit will follow. If the audio doesn't work, no amount of
   B-roll saves it.
4. **Then handle picture** — cover jump cuts and tighten with B-roll on V2 (the
   A-roll + its audio stay continuous on V1/A1; see the B-roll rule in `fundamentals`).

## Cutting at word boundaries (precision)

Transcript word timestamps are accurate to ~50–100ms. Apply padding so you don't
clip consonants or leave gaps:

| Cut style | Start padding (before first word) | End padding (after last word) | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tight** (default) | 15–25 ms | 5–10 ms | clean professional cuts; user says "tight/punchy" |
| **Safe** | 50 ms | 25 ms | rough cuts, first pass, less critical |

- **Never cut mid-word** unless deliberate.
- Cut on the **down-slope of the breath** at the end of a sentence — the natural seam.
- If a cut clips a word's hard consonant (t, k, p, b), nudge the end padding out a few ms.
- If you hear a gap/"space"/"bleeding," tighten the padding; if words are clipped, loosen.

## Removing filler — but keep humanity

Cut: long "um/uh," false starts, repeated words, rambling tangents, dead air over
~0.6–0.8s mid-thought, and anything that doesn't add meaning or feeling.

**Do NOT cut everything.** A surgically de-ummed edit where every micro-pause is
removed sounds robotic and anxious. Leave:
- the **thinking beat** before an emotional or important answer,
- a **breath** after a punchline or a heavy statement,
- the occasional natural "you know" that conveys personality.

The goal is *the person at their most articulate*, not a machine reading a transcript.

## BREATHING ROOM (the seconds between things)

Breathing room is the deliberate space — pauses, holds, silence — that lets the
viewer absorb and feel. It is the difference between an edit that *lands* and one
that *exhausts*. Machine-gun pacing with zero air is the most common amateur tell.

**Where to leave air:**
- **After a strong/emotional statement** — hold the speaker (or cut to a reaction)
  for ~0.5–1.5s of silence. The pause *is* the emphasis. Cutting away instantly
  throws away the impact.
- **Before a big answer** — keep the thinking beat; the anticipation has value.
- **At section/topic transitions** — a short held shot or a beat of ambience signals
  "new idea" and lets the previous one settle.
- **On a laugh or a reaction** — let it play; don't cut on the first frame of a smile.
- **At the end** — let the final line breathe before the cut to black.

**How much air (rules of thumb):**
- Conversational pause between thoughts: **~0.3–0.6s** feels natural.
- Emphasis/emotional hold: **~0.8–1.5s.**
- Comedic timing: the pause *before* the punchline lands the setup; the pause *after*
  lets the audience laugh — both ~0.5–1.0s.
- Dramatic/heavy moment: up to **2–3s** of held silence is powerful when earned.

**The contrast principle applies to air, too.** Tight, quick exchanges make a single
held pause hit hard. If everything has air, nothing breathes. Vary it: tighten the
informational parts, open up the emotional ones.

**A practical test:** watch the cut and breathe with it. If you feel rushed or can't
keep up, you've removed too much air. If your attention drifts, you've left too much.

## J-cuts and L-cuts (make dialogue invisible)

Dialogue edits should almost never have audio and picture cut on the same frame:
- **L-cut:** let the current speaker's audio continue a beat *over* the cut to the
  listener/B-roll. Smooths the transition; shows reaction.
- **J-cut:** bring the next speaker's audio in *before* their picture. Pulls the
  conversation forward; feels natural.
Use these on nearly every speaker change. They're the signature of a pro dialogue edit.

## B-roll over interviews

- B-roll on V2 covers jump cuts AND illustrates what's said — align cutaways to the
  relevant line using transcript timestamps.
- Don't B-roll over the speaker's **most emotional on-camera moments** — we want to
  see their face there. Save cutaways for informational or list-y passages.
- Return to the speaker's face for punchlines and emotional peaks.

## Captions / subtitles

- For social or accessibility, caption key lines (or all). Time caption changes to
  word boundaries; never change caption text mid-pause or across a visual cut.
- Keep 1–2 lines; break at natural phrase boundaries, not mid-clause.

## Multi-camera interviews

- Cut between angles on natural beats (a new sentence, a gesture, a topic shift) —
  not randomly. An angle change should feel motivated.
- Use a wider/2-shot for context, push to the close for emotion.
- Keep the 180° line; don't flip screen direction between angles of the same person.

## Interview/dialogue self-review

- [ ] Does the radio edit (audio only) tell a coherent story?
- [ ] Filler removed, but thinking beats / emotional breaths preserved?
- [ ] Is there breathing room after the heaviest moments (not cut away instantly)?
- [ ] Pacing varies — tight on info, open on emotion?
- [ ] Speaker changes softened with J/L cuts?
- [ ] B-roll illustrates the words and covers jump cuts (not over emotional faces)?
- [ ] Captions (if any) aligned to word boundaries and visual cuts?
